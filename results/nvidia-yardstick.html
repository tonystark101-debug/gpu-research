Our core NVIDIA core pale bar: its maximum on paper

Our fix took our core from 39 to 74: level with an L4 core and about half a T4 core, the Turing chip our plan targets. We can't simulate big jobs yet. On our largest run (256 × 256 × 256) we stay at 75, while a T4 core does 271 on the same size.

Why small jobs fall short: memory. Every NVIDIA core sits right at its memory's speed limit.

A small job needs a lot of data for little maths, so memory sets the pace, not the AI unit. The L4 edges past its line because its large on-chip cache (48 MB) holds some of the data. Our simulator gives our one core 20–115× more memory speed than an NVIDIA core gets, so our small-job result is flattered until we re-test it on realistic memory.

Attention, the heart of AI chat models, is our biggest gap: 800–2,300× behind.

Our core NVIDIA core

Our attention test runs 5.7 million ordinary instructions to do 2.1 million multiply-adds, so our AI unit spends almost all its time waiting. NVIDIA gets much faster on long text, up to 1,460 per clock. We haven't run long text yet.

Smaller numbers, more work: 8-bit maths nearly doubles the work per clock on chips built for it.

Big job (4,096 × 4,096 × 4,096). Multiply-adds per clock tick, for one core, with the share of its maximum in grey. A dash means the chip has no hardware for that format.

The AI unit is 5–19× faster than the general maths cores (the fp32 row). int8 came out almost the same on all four chips, whatever their hardware can do, so the limit is the software route we used, not the chips: software matters even for NVIDIA. Our core: only fp16 tested so far.

Under heavy load, power sets the pace, not top speed.

To stay inside its power limit, a T4 slows to 43% of its top speed on a big job; even an H100 slows to 65%. Work per watt (TFLOPS: trillions of maths operations a second) rose 2.6× from the T4 (2018) to the H100 (2022). Our core's predicted top speed is 840 MHz, but that's on an academic 7 nm-class model, not a real factory's process. Its power isn't known yet.

What this tells us

  • The yardstick works. NVIDIA's chips hit 88–95% of their published maximum on big jobs, so these numbers can be trusted.
  • Against a Turing core, our plan's target: 1.9× behind on small jobs, 3.6× on medium ones, and about 940× on attention.
  • Small jobs are a memory race for everyone. Feeding data matters more than adding maths units, which fits our data-movement bet.
  • Power, not clock speed, limits real chips. Work per watt is the number to design for.
